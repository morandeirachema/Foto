# Small, masked moves rescue Namibia's JPEGs

The best way to edit these 2,731 Sony a6700 JPEGs is to treat each file as already developed: make few, small, mostly masked tonal and colour moves in one non-destructive pass in Luminar, add almost no sharpening or noise reduction on top of what the camera baked in, and export once in sRGB. Clipped highlights are gone, shadows tolerate roughly +1 to +2 EV, and large white-balance swings fail, so the three hardest groups in this set (underexposed dusk frames, orange floodlit waterhole frames, and the underexposed Milky Way) need a change of interpretation or an external step, not stronger sliders. For the night sky that external step is stacking on the Mac, because Skylum documents no frame-averaging tool; Starry Landscape Stacker is the Sequator equivalent, with Siril as the free alternative. The existing Spanish guide is sound on tool names and already cautious about JPEG, but it contains two clear factual conflicts with Skylum's documentation (white-balance presets on a JPEG; "only the shortcut shows the original"), several wording or scope conflicts, and it omits documented features that matter for exactly this set: the Edits tab, the per-channel histogram, masked Supersharp AI, Home Printing with soft proofing, and licence gating. Its night chapter also tells the reader to remove green from the sky, which in Bortle 1-2 Namibia is most likely natural airglow. Evidence quality varies: almost every expert source assumes RAW, no controlled JPEG test exists for any step, and all numeric JPEG limits below are researcher extrapolations. Throughout this report a claim is sourced from a page the researchers read unless it is marked "(snippet)", meaning it came from a search-result summary only, or is explicitly called researcher inference; observations about the owner's photos come from the coordinator's first-hand notes in `research_notes/Editing safari landscape night JPEGs/owner_sample_photos.md`.

## The 8-bit file leaves about two stops of shadow and no highlights

### What the evidence supports, and how thin it is

No rigorous measurement of JPEG editing latitude in stops was found for the a6700 or any current camera. The evidence is informal. A one-stop-step test found that "at 3-stops under, the corrected JPG still looks okay" while RAW was clearly better "even at just one stop over" ([Photodoto](https://photodoto.com/raw-vs-jpg-correcting-underover-exposure)); that test dates from about 2006. A June 2026 four-camera field test found RAW visibly ahead in only one of four scenes, a severely underexposed bird in flight, where the pushed JPEG showed edge colour distortion and a dirtier result after denoising, while a heavily underexposed canyon scene "recovered cleanly from the JPEG" ([Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/education/raw-vs-jpeg-grand-canyon-what-four-cameras-actually-showed-903184)). From these the researchers infer planning figures for this camera: **treat anything at 255 as gone, +1 to +2 EV of shadow lift as generally safe, +3 EV as the edge**. That is extrapolation, not measurement.

White balance is the second hard limit. The camera applies it to linear sensor data and then adds non-linear rendering and 8-bit clipping, so a later correction is an approximation (snippet: [York University](https://yorkucvil.github.io/projects/public_html/ColorTemperatureTuning/files/ColorTemperatureTuning.pdf); [arXiv 2004.01354](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.01354)). Moderate corrections work in practice: a Nikon Z9 JPEG pushed blue by auto white balance "corrected well with a straightforward adjustment" ([Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/education/raw-vs-jpeg-grand-canyon-what-four-cameras-actually-showed-903184)). No source says how far a shift can go before artefacts show. This matters for the owner's set because **91% of frames carry a fixed white balance**, which produced systematic casts: cool in dusk shade, orange under floodlights, brown-olive at night. The upside, also first-hand, is that a fixed cast is identical across a session, so one correction can be synced to many frames.

Banding is the third. "Any process which 'stretches' the histogram has the potential to cause posterization", smooth skies show it first, and per-channel histograms are the most sensitive check ([Cambridge in Colour](https://cdn.cambridgeincolour.com/tutorials/posterization.htm)). JPEG quantisation causes banding independently of bit depth, errors stay inside 8×8 blocks (16×16 with chroma subsampling), and chroma is stored at reduced resolution ([Cloudinary](https://cloudinary.com/blog/why_jpeg_is_like_a_photocopier)). Sharpening amplifies compression artefacts ([Cambridge in Colour](https://cdn.cambridgeincolour.com/tutorials/image-sharpening.htm)). The researchers infer the practical consequences: dehaze, structure and strong clarity work at the scale of block edges and ringing, saturation pushes the most heavily quantised data, and in-camera noise reduction removes the natural grain that would otherwise hide banding, which is why adding fine grain after tonal edits is a legitimate remedy. One source calls added noise a workaround, not a fix ([Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/gear/precision-myth-photographers-guide-bit-depth-719847)).

The baked-in processing is strong on this camera. DPReview describes base-ISO JPEG sharpening as "decent though boarding on aggressive" ([DPReview](https://www.dpreview.com/reviews/sony-a6700-review)). The sourced rules that follow are that noise reduction always precedes sharpening and that output sharpening comes last, after resizing ([Cambridge in Colour](https://cdn.cambridgeincolour.com/tutorials/image-sharpening.htm)). The inference is that global capture-style sharpening should be near zero and that strong luminance denoising of a camera-denoised file turns plastic quickly.

### The order of decisions

No source gives a tested JPEG-specific order; the sequence below is assembled by the researchers from the sourced rules above plus the genre workflows, and in a non-destructive editor it governs strength more than sequence. Cull first. Then crop and straighten, trim white balance by a small amount, set global tone with one gentle move per control, denoise lightly with chroma first, do local work through masks, adjust colour with vibrance or targeted hue controls, add local contrast sparingly and off the sky, sharpen selectively if at all, add grain where gradients are at risk, and resize, sharpen for output and export once. Each re-encode adds loss and the number of generations matters more than the quality setting ([Cloudinary](https://cloudinary.com/blog/why_jpeg_is_like_a_photocopier)); Skylum says the same: "You should generally only save JPEG files once" ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/saving-and-exporting-files/export-image)). Luminar stores edits as a `.state` file per image with unlimited history and links to originals without moving them ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/saving-and-exporting-files/saving-adjustments-in-a-catalog)), so the minimum of two generations is what the owner already gets.

Keep everything in sRGB. Converting an 8-bit sRGB file to a wider space adds no colour, and wide-gamut spaces raise posterization risk at the same bit depth ([Cambridge in Colour](https://cdn.cambridgeincolour.com/tutorials/posterization.htm)). For prints, resolution is not the constraint: by the researchers' arithmetic an uncropped 6192×4128 frame gives about **349 ppi at 30×45 cm**, and a crop to roughly 4252×2835 still gives 240 ppi. The limit is artefacts, which enlarge with the print and hide on a phone. Informal tests put "visually lossless" export around quality 77-85 on Adobe's scale with sky gradients as the exception ([Jeffrey Friedl](https://regex.info/blog/lightroom-goodies/jpeg-quality)); scales do not transfer between applications, and Skylum documents neither its Quality scale nor its export Sharpen levels ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/saving-and-exporting-files/export-image)).

Two set-wide jobs come before any creative editing. The first is culling: 68% of the frames were shot on two days and about 1,000 fall in the 19:00 hour. The Toons' method is a fast reject pass, then a second pass comparing similar frames "for small differences, such as pricked ears or alert expressions", with a binary keep or reject ([Australian Photography](https://www.australianphotography.com/photo-tips/lightroom-tips-for-wildlife-photographers-part-one)). The second is sensor dust: the same upper-left spots appear in at least three daylight frames from different days, at f/6.3 to f/11. Luminar documents Erase with Objects Removal → Remove Dust Spots ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/essential-tools/erase)). Pushing dehaze to maximum temporarily is a known way to reveal faint spots (snippet: [Have Camera Will Travel](https://havecamerawilltravel.com/lightroom/dehaze-dust-spots/)); applying that trick with Luminar's Landscape → Dehaze is a researcher adaptation. The coordinator's note that a synced spot removal is "plausible" collides with Skylum's statement that Erase cannot be synced ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/how-to-use-luminar-for-desktop/catalog/syncing-adjustments)); assume dust removal is per frame until tested in the app.

## Safari frames need tone and colour separation, not rescue

### Consensus workflow and where it bends for JPEG

The wildlife sources converge on: cull, crop early, fix technical flaws, set global tone and white balance with small moves, then use masks to lead the eye to the face and separate animal from background, treat the eye, and sharpen only the subject. Mark Dumbleton removes noise before anything else "so you don't enhance artifacts later" ([Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/lightroom/stop-guessing-professional-wildlife-editing-guide-900221)). Steve and Ann Toon's typical magnitudes are small even on RAW: exposure within 0.25 stop, Shadows and Blacks rarely beyond ±15, Clarity about +10 masked to the animal, Vibrance rarely above +10, Tint never beyond ±10 ([Australian Photography](https://www.australianphotography.com/photo-tips/lightroom-tips-for-wildlife-photographers-part-two)). The researchers read those as upper bounds for JPEG. Matt Kloskowski supplies the stop conditions: avoid the "sticker effect" where the animal looks pasted in, avoid pushing background texture negative into "a ghostly smear", and judge at 100% and at the size the picture will be seen ([Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/nature/what-separates-natural-wildlife-edit-overcooked-one-905317)).

Noise handling on JPEG is the same technique applied more gently. Adobe says photos already strongly denoised in camera show less improvement from AI denoise (snippet: [Adobe](https://helpx.adobe.com/in/camera-raw/using/enhance.html)). Sources agree on applying noise reduction mainly to the background and sharpening only the subject ([Nature TTL](https://www.naturettl.com/?p=5048); [Digital Photography School](https://digital-photography-school.com/5-unforgiving-post-processing-mistakes-every-bird-photographer-must-avoid)). Skylum documents Noiseless AI as working on non-raw files with "optimal results when applied to raw photos", and warns that its Details slider "can reintroduce noise or create artifacts" ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/image-quality-tools/noiseless-ai)). No independent test of any AI denoiser on high-ISO camera JPEGs was found. For this set, where **27% of frames exceed ISO 1600 and 10% exceed ISO 3200**, the inference with the most practical weight is that downsizing to phone size is itself a strong denoiser, so noise should be judged at output size before reaching for heavy settings.

### The five safari situations in this set

The dusk frames are the bulk of the work. The lion at 19:00 (DSC03658, ISO 2000) shows a cool blue-grey cast and low contrast in open shade; the cheetah (DSC02462) is underexposed and cool. The Toons warm animals shot in shadow gently with temperature, moving a leopard from 5700 to 6254 K, and avoid the eyedropper "since it can remove the warmth of the light" ([Australian Photography](https://www.australianphotography.com/photo-tips/lightroom-tips-for-wildlife-photographers-part-two)). On a JPEG in Luminar only the eyedropper, Temperature and Tint are documented; the White Balance preset menu is documented for raw files ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/essential-tools/develop-raw-develop)). The recommended treatment, as synthesis: correct one frame per session with a modest warm shift and a lift inside the +1 to +2 EV envelope, sync it across the session with Sync Adjustments, then do crop and masks per frame, since Crop cannot be synced ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/how-to-use-luminar-for-desktop/catalog/syncing-adjustments)).

Small subjects set the output. The cheetah fills about 10% of the frame width and needs a 2-3× linear crop. Sources warn that huge crops "leave a very small file and magnify noise and soft focus" ([Nature TTL](https://www.naturettl.com/?p=5048)) and ask for space in the direction the animal faces ([Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/nature/what-separates-natural-wildlife-edit-overcooked-one-905317)). By arithmetic, half the frame width (3096 px) prints about 26 cm wide at 300 ppi and a third (2064 px) about 26 cm at 200 ppi, so such crops are phone and small-print images. Luminar's Upscale offers 2x, 4x and 6x, and Skylum advises noise reduction before upscaling and sharpening after ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/luminar-for-desktop-tips/best-practices-with-upscale)). Upscaled detail is synthesised; Wildlife Photographer of the Year tells entrants "Please do not upscale" ([NHM](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/visit/wpy/competition/adult-competition/rules.html)). Reserve it for prints of moderate crops.

The floodlit waterhole frames cannot be neutralised. The elephants at 01:00 (DSC01526, ISO 12800, 1/25 s) show an extreme orange monochrome cast, blocked blacks and a probably clipped red channel; 174 frames were shot between 22:00 and 02:00. Photographers report that Okaukuejo's floodlights give very orange results that are easily corrected only from RAW (snippet: [Morden-O'Hare](https://morden-ohare.squarespace.com/blog/2014/7/11/namibias-etosha-national-park)). No expert tutorial on editing such frames was found, for RAW or JPEG, and the lamp type is unknown. The options are therefore all researcher inference: a partial cooling that stops when the animals no longer look uniformly orange, reducing orange saturation selectively in HSL in place of a large temperature move, a black-and-white conversion that sidesteps the cast and hides chroma noise, or accepting the orange as documentary truth. Luminar documents the needed tools: the per-channel histogram to confirm red clipping ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/how-to-use-luminar-for-desktop/catalog/histogram)), Color → HSL and the Remove Color Cast slider ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/essential-tools/color)), and Black & White ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/essential-tools/black-and-white)). At 1/25 s on moving animals, cull for motion blur first.

Flat midday frames such as the giraffe (DSC01825) want moderate contrast and a crop, which also removes the orange blur in the corner. Heat shimmer is refraction, and "images with severe or even moderate heat distortion can't be fixed" ([Adorama](https://www.adorama.com/alc/how-to-avoid-heat-distortion-in-photography/)); reject those at the cull. The Toons rarely use dehaze globally ([Australian Photography](https://www.australianphotography.com/photo-tips/lightroom-tips-for-wildlife-photographers-part-two)). For harsh contrast that cannot be balanced, a working safari guide leans into it and marks the frame for black and white ([Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/education/can-still-get-good-wildlife-shots-harsh-midday-light-902576)).

Finally, the honesty line. Wildlife Photographer of the Year permits tone, dodging, burning, cropping, sharpening and noise reduction and forbids "adding, moving or removing objects, animals or parts of animals" and "any post-processing that adds new content through AI" ([NHM](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/visit/wpy/competition/adult-competition/rules.html)). The guide already follows this. Skylum's own pet-photography tip recommends Gaussian blur on a background mask and GenErase ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/luminar-for-desktop-tips/best-practices-in-pet-photography)); no wildlife source recommends artificial blur, and the researchers judge it a "pasted-in" trigger, so documented availability is not a recommendation.

## Landscapes: protect the sky gradient and the red channel

The landscape sources share one shape: geometry and crop, global tone, colour, local adjustments, then noise and sharpening sized to the output. William Patino's version is "Light, Colour, Local", with vibrance preferred to saturation and edits left to "marinate" ([Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/landscapes/simple-workflow-better-landscape-photo-edits-679023)). Rick Ohnsman balances sky and land with a linear gradient refined by luminance range, or an inverted sky mask ([Digital Photography School](https://digital-photography-school.com/post-processing-workflow-tips-landscape-photography/)). None of the sources addresses JPEG or desert colour, and no fetched material from several leading landscape educators was obtained, so the adaptations here are inference.

The Deadvlei frame (DSC00149: 11 mm, f/11, ISO 100) is the model case. It has three dust spots in the upper-left sky, a backlit person and tree in shadow against a sunlit orange dune, a blue cast on the shadowed clay, and a clean sky gradient. The order that follows from the evidence: remove dust first; lift the shadowed subject through a mask, staying near one stop; leave the sky almost alone. Dehaze, saturation and vibrance pushed too far trigger sky banding (snippet: [ExpertPhotography](https://expertphotography.com/color-banding)), and saturation drives oranges and reds to the limit first so skies and sand turn "crunchy" ([Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/lightroom/vibrance-vs-saturation-mistake-makes-sunsets-look-fake-719262)). No source measured red-channel clipping in dune or sunset JPEGs; the researchers' advice is to check the red channel in the per-channel histogram before adding warmth or saturation, and to prefer slightly lowering orange saturation plus local contrast over raising it. Their suggested JPEG ceilings (hue about ±10, saturation about ±15, luminance about ±10-15 on a Lightroom-type scale) are rules of thumb, and Skylum publishes no slider scales to map them onto. The white clay pan serves as a neutral reference: if it reads pink or orange, the balance is too warm (inference). Removing other tourists falls under "removing small and transient elements", which the Natural Landscape Photography Awards allow, while adding skies, sun or mist is prohibited ([NLPA](https://www.naturallandscapeawards.com/rules/)).

Halos at dune crests come from masks or adjustments that are too strong at a hard edge (snippet: [Greg Benz](https://gregbenzphotography.com/luminosity-masking/how-to-remove-exposure-blending-halos/)). Luminar documents Linear Gradient, Mask AI with a sky category, and Luminosity masks, plus Feather among the mask actions ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/masking-options)). Haze deserves restraint for a second reason specific to place: distant dust gives the receding layers their depth, so dehaze belongs on the far band only, at low values (inference).

Lens corrections need no action. With the Sony E 11mm f/1.8 the camera's distortion compensation is locked on, and JPEG distortion measured −0.75% against −9.60% uncorrected (snippet: [LensTip](https://lenstip.com/631.6-Lens_review-Sony_E_11_mm_f_1.8_Distortion_and_field_of_view.html)). Luminar's Auto Distortion Corrections and Auto Fix Chromatic Aberrations are documented as raw-only ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/essential-tools/develop-raw-develop)), so accidental double correction is not possible on these files; only the manual Lens Distortion and Devignette sliders remain. The metadata shows 95% of frames from the 70-350 and 5% from the 11 mm, with none from the 25 mm, so the landscape set is small and partly telephoto.

For print, Luminar 1.29 added Home Printing with ICC profiles, soft proofing, Print Sharpening (None/Low/Medium/High) and a print-ready "Save as File" option, and Order Prints with a low-resolution warning ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/printing/home-printing); [Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/printing/order-prints)). Soft proofing does not replace a test print (snippet: [Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/printing/how-create-better-photo-prints-610897)). Merges from JPEG are unverified on both sides: Skylum's HDR Merge, Panorama Stitching and Focus Stacking pages state no input formats ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/extra-tools/hdr-merge)), and no source tests merge quality from JPEG.

## Night sky: stack on the Mac first, then stretch gently

### Why the JPEG night frames are the hardest case

The set holds 53 exposures of 8 s or longer, all at 11 mm, typically 15-20 s at f/1.8-2.2 and ISO 1600-3200. Every expert tutorial found assumes RAW; none describes a JPEG Milky Way workflow. Ian Norman's order is exposure, white balance, subtle contrast, highlights and shadows, presence, a gentle S-curve with the black point raised slightly and no clipping, and noise reduction last, with an example push of +2.45 stops ([Lonely Speck](https://www.lonelyspeck.com/how-to-process-milky-way-astrophotography-in-adobe-lightroom/)). Siril's documentation says 8-bit JPEG "must therefore generally be used at the end of the processing chain" ([Siril](https://siril.readthedocs.io/en/stable/file-formats/Common.html)). The researchers' adaptation: a dark sky occupies a handful of the 256 levels, so treat about +1 stop as the point to start inspecting at 100%, use a curve in place of a large exposure push, and take roughly half of the RAW presence ceilings. Those figures are estimates.

The Milky Way frame (DSC00762, 20 s, f/2, ISO 1600) is clearly underexposed with a near-black sky. It needs the largest lift in the set from the weakest part of the file. Matt Suess keeps a dehaze-type filter "around plus 12" because more "starts outlining the stars", and warns that "halos are the giveaway of an over-processed night sky" ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2026/06/24/how-to-turn-a-flat-noisy-raw-into-a-finished-milky-way-photograph/)); that article is DxO-sponsored and RAW-based. Realistic targets from these files are a clean, moderately contrasty Milky Way for screens and small to medium prints, not the deep dust-lane contrast of RAW or tracked work (inference).

### Colour: the green band is probably real

The other viewed night frame (DSC00056) renders brown-olive with green near the horizon and a small white light dome. Roger Clark argues that "a daylight white balance is the correct white balance to show the true colors" of the night sky, that green airglow comes from atomic oxygen at 557.7 nm, that Milky Way dust is "a brownish red", and that "too many nightscapes are artificially turned blue in post processing" ([Clarkvision](https://clarkvision.com/articles/color.of.the.night.sky)). Norman's files converge on about 3900 K, a cooler rendering ([Lonely Speck](https://www.lonelyspeck.com/how-to-process-milky-way-astrophotography-in-adobe-lightroom/)). The disagreement is physical accuracy against popular taste. For a documentary album from a Bortle 1-2 site, the defensible middle is a neutral-grey to slightly warm sky, a warm tan core, and the green band kept, perhaps slightly desaturated, with the localised white dome toned down through a mask. That synthesis is inference; the physics behind it is sourced. Skylum's documentation has no astro content at all: the only mention of night skies in 175 pages is that Sky Enhancer AI "is not designed to work with night skies" ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/essential-tools/enhance-ai)).

### Stacking tools for macOS

Skylum documents no mean or median stacking; its merge tools are HDR Merge, Focus Stacking and Panorama Stitching ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/how-to-use-luminar-for-desktop/luminar-for-desktop-walkthrough)). A companion app is unavoidable. Stacking requires frames "captured in rapid succession with identical exposure settings" from a motionless tripod ([Starry Landscape Stacker](https://sites.google.com/site/starrylandscapestacker/home)); whether the 53 long exposures include such sequences must be checked in the catalog. The expected gain is inference from standard statistics: ten frames improve signal-to-noise by about 3.2×, somewhat less on JPEGs because in-camera noise reduction correlates the noise, and the 16-bit average of 8-bit frames contains real intermediate levels, which directly reduces banding when stretched.

The recommended path, as researcher inference: try the Starry Landscape Stacker trial on the JPEGs directly; if rejected, convert them unedited to 16-bit TIFF, stack, export a 16-bit TIFF, and do the whole stretch on that file in Luminar, which accepts 8- and 16-bit TIFF ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/about-luminar-for-desktop/supported-file-types-and-cameras)). Apply no lens correction, denoise or sharpening before stacking; lens profile corrections before stacking can cause irreparable moiré ([National Parks at Night](https://www.nationalparksatnight.com/blog/2018/8/18/four-ways-to-remove-airplanes-from-star-trails-in-post-production)). One free diagnostic is available now: the a6700's Long Exposure NR runs for exposures of 1 s or longer and for the duration of the exposure ([Sony](https://helpguide.sony.net/ilc/2320/v1/en/contents/0414_long_exposure_nr.html)), so a gap between consecutive frames roughly equal to the exposure time shows it was on, which also means dark frames are unnecessary (inference).

Single frames get light, late denoising. Suess deliberately keeps "a touch of grain" to preserve stars ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2026/06/24/how-to-turn-a-flat-noisy-raw-into-a-finished-milky-way-photograph/)); AI denoisers are reported to create swirl artefacts between stars (snippet: [Adobe Community](https://community.adobe.com/t5/lightroom-classic-discussions/ai-denoise-creates-swirl-artifacts-astrophotography/m-p/14787212)). How Noiseless AI treats stars is undocumented and untested. Foregrounds in these frames are pure black; keeping acacias and buildings as silhouettes is both the honest and the technically safe choice (inference). A same-place, same-time stack is not a composite under the standards checked ([NLPA](https://www.naturallandscapeawards.com/rules/)), though captioning "10-frame stack" is good practice.

## One twelve-step base pass fits every photo

The genre workflows above share a common skeleton, set out here as a base pass to run on every kept frame. It follows the guide's existing order (noise, crop, light, colour, texture, local work, finish) so the two stay compatible. The sequence itself is researcher synthesis: no source tested an order on JPEG input, and only two ordering rules are sourced, noise reduction before sharpening and output sharpening last ([Cambridge in Colour](https://cdn.cambridgeincolour.com/tutorials/image-sharpening.htm)). Every Luminar control named is documented by Skylum on the pages cited earlier in this report; the amounts are deliberately described in words, because Skylum publishes no slider scales. Steps 4, 6 and 7 are the ones to set once on a representative frame and sync across a session shot in the same light; steps 3, 5 and 8 are per frame, since Skylum excludes Crop and Erase from Sync. Night sequences get one step before all of this: stack first, then run the pass on the stacked TIFF.

| Step | What to do | Luminar control | Stop when | Skip or change it when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Decide | Name the subject, the distraction and what the light really was. Decide the output: phone, or print | None; 30 seconds of looking | You can say what the edit is for | Never skip. If the frame is heat-shimmered, misfocused or motion-blurred, reject it here |
| 2. Measure | Check clipping and casts before touching anything | `J` clipping indicators; View > Show Histogram, clicked through R, G and B | You know which ends and which channels are already at the wall | A channel at the wall (red on dunes or floodlit animals) means no added warmth or saturation later |
| 3. Dust | Remove sensor spots in the sky, upper left first in this set | Erase → Objects Removal → Remove Dust Spots, refined with Erase and Restore | Sky is clean at 100% | No sky in frame. Re-check after step 9 |
| 4. Noise | Light denoise, colour noise first, only if noise shows at the size the photo will be seen | Noiseless AI at the suggested level or lower, or Denoise; Develop also has its own Noise Reduction | Colour blotches calm down and fur or stars still have texture | Low ISO: skip. Stars: lower strength until faint stars return |
| 5. Crop | Straighten, then crop leaving space where the animal looks; cut limbs fully or not at all | Crop AI (`C`) | The horizon is level and the subject has room | Keep 3:2 for 10×15, 20×30 and 30×45 prints |
| 6. White balance | A small trim toward what the light was; keep golden-hour and firelight warmth | Develop → Color: Temperature and Tint, or the eyedropper (`W`) on a mid-grey, not near-white, area | Neutral things look neutral and the mood survives | Floodlit and night frames: correct only part of the way |
| 7. Light | Exposure for the subject, then Highlights and Shadows, then Whites and Blacks to the first clipping warning | Develop → Light and Blacks & Whites; one gentle Curve if needed | The subject reads correctly; the lift stays within about one to two stops | Clipped areas stay clipped: stop pulling when they turn grey. Silhouette instead of forcing a lift |
| 8. Local | Slightly brighten the subject or face, slightly darken or mute what competes, treat the eye last | Dodge & Burn; or a tool masked with Object Select AI, Mask AI, Radial or Linear Gradient, with Feather | The eye goes to the subject and nothing looks pasted in | Keep the difference across any mask edge small; halos mean too much |
| 9. Colour and texture | Vibrance before Saturation; calm one colour in HSL if it shouts; local contrast on the subject or land only | Color (Vibrance, HSL); Structure AI or Details, masked; Landscape → Dehaze masked to the distant band | Colours match memory; no halos at edges | Never on clear sky or the star background |
| 10. Finish | Fine grain if a gradient shows bands; a mild vignette if edges are bright | Film Grain; Vignette (Skylum advises not below −50 for realism) | Bands are disguised and the vignette is not noticeable as such | No banding and quiet edges: skip both |
| 11. Check | Toggle before and after; inspect at 100% and at output size; look again the next day | `\`, the Visibility icon or the per-tool eye; `Cmd+1` | The edit does not shout; if it does, halve it | Never skip for prints |
| 12. Export | Resize for the use, output sharpening last, one export from the original | Export (`Shift+Cmd+E`), sRGB; Home Printing for paper | One file per use: about 2,000 px long edge for sharing, full size for print | Smooth sky gradient: choose a higher quality |

The checks in step 11 are the sourced part of the routine: view at 100% and at the size the image will be seen, and leave the edit overnight ([Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/nature/what-separates-natural-wildlife-edit-overcooked-one-905317)). The faults to look for are the same across genres: halos at subject edges or the horizon, waxy fur, grey whites, neon grass or orange lions, a cut-out subject, bands or blocks in the sky, and at night a saturated blue sky or ringed stars. Under the 8-bit budget of the first section, the base pass should usually finish in fewer moves than it has steps: most daylight frames need steps 3, 5, 6, 7 and 12 and little else.

### What the picture contains decides which steps matter

The base pass is the same for every frame; its emphasis is not. The table below starts from what is visible in the picture and gives the steps in the order to do them, using the step numbers above. The content types are the ones found in the owner's set, with the viewed frame that represents each. The routes are researcher synthesis from the genre evidence in the earlier sections; the tools are Skylum-documented, the amounts are not, and a picture that contains two of these things (an animal under a dusty sky, a person in front of a dune) takes both rows.

| If the picture contains | Do these steps, in this order | Leave alone |
|---|---|---|
| **An animal, fair size, in shade or at dusk** (lion, DSC03658) | 4 light noise → 5 moderate crop with space ahead of the head → 6 warm Temperature a little → 7 lift Exposure and Shadows modestly → 8 Object Select AI on the animal: slight brightness and Structure AI; inverted mask: slightly darker or less saturated background; small Dodge & Burn on the iris → 11 check for a pasted-in look | The eyedropper on warm light; background blur; sharpening the background |
| **A small, distant animal** (cheetah, DSC02462) | 1 decide the output is phone or small print → 4 noise before cropping, judged at output size → 5 crop 2-3× linear, eye on a third, room in the direction it faces → 6 and 7 as above → 8 keep the subject-background difference small, since mask edges are rough on small animals → 12 export at about 2,000 px; Upscale 2x only for a print, with noise reduction before and sharpening after | Eye sharpening when the eye is only a few pixels wide; 4x or 6x Upscale |
| **An animal in flat midday light or haze** (giraffe, DSC01825) | 3 dust in the sky → 5 crop to move the subject off centre and cut edge distractions → 7 Smart Contrast or a gentle S-curve → 9 Dehaze at a low value, masked to the hazy distance; Vibrance, not Saturation → 3 again: re-check dust | Golden Hour or added warmth on flat light; heavy Dehaze; if the contrast cannot be balanced, convert to Black & White instead |
| **An animal under floodlights at night** (elephants, DSC01526) | 1 reject motion-blurred frames → 2 look at the red channel → 4 Noiseless AI, stronger on the background than on the hide → 6 cool only part of the way → 9 lower Orange saturation in HSL, or switch to Black & White → 7 leave the surroundings black → 12 share small | Full neutralising; shadow lifting; sharpening; printing large |
| **Blue sky above the subject, shot at f/6.3-f/11** (any daylight frame) | 3 Remove Dust Spots, upper left first → 7 and 8 darken the sky only slightly, with a soft Linear Gradient or the Mask AI sky → 10 Film Grain if bands appear → 11 inspect the sky at 100% | Structure AI, Details or Dehaze on the sky; lowering Blue luminance far |
| **Red dunes or orange sand** | 2 check the red channel first → 7 tone before colour → 9 add local contrast for ripples; lower Orange saturation slightly if texture has gone flat → 11 inspect dune crests against the sky for halos and fringes | Raising Orange or Red saturation when red is at the wall; large Hue moves, which split the dune into two colours |
| **A person or tree backlit, bright scene behind** (Deadvlei, DSC00149) | 3 dust → 7 set the whole frame for the bright dune and sky → 8 lift only the shadowed subject through a mask, by about a stop at most; trim the blue cast in the shadowed clay with the same mask → optional: Erase on distant tourists, which counts as a small, transient element | Lifting Shadows across the whole frame; if the lift shows noise, keep the subject as a silhouette |
| **Sunset, sunrise or a deliberate silhouette** | 5 level the true horizon → 6 keep the warmth → 7 lower Whites slightly near the sun; let silhouettes go black → 9 Vibrance in small amounts → 10 Film Grain for the sky gradient | Neutral white balance; Exposure raised near the sun; Sky AI or Sunrays if the picture is presented as what was seen |
| **Stars or the Milky Way** (DSC00762, DSC00056) | 0 stack the sequence first if one exists → 6 small shift toward a neutral-grey or slightly warm sky; keep green airglow → 7 lift with a Curve, inspecting at 100% from about one stop → 8 Radial mask on the core: modest contrast and Structure AI → 4 light denoise last, keeping some grain → 10 Film Grain against banding → 11 check stars for rings and the sky for bands | Sky Enhancer AI; global Dehaze or Structure AI; sharpening the sky; a blue-saturated sky; lifting the black foreground |
| **Fire, lanterns or lodge lights** | 6 keep the light warm; if the sky matters, cool it separately through a mask → 7 lower Whites slightly; let the flame core stay bright → 9 lower Orange saturation a little for texture in lit faces and ground → 10 grain in the fall-off around the light | Dragging Highlights far down on clipped flames, which turns them grey; sharpening flames or sparks |

Read as a whole, the table shows a pattern worth teaching directly in the guide: content decides three things, namely where the mask goes (step 8), which colour is at risk (step 2 and step 9), and what output the frame can support (step 1 and step 12). Everything else in the pass stays constant.

### How to create a mask, from zero

The two tables above say "through a mask" many times and assume the reader knows how. This is the procedure as Skylum documents it. A mask limits one tool's effect to part of the picture: the tool still has the same sliders, but they act only where the mask is. The guide's chapter 5 (`retoques/05-ajustes-locales.md`) covers the same ground in Spanish with worked cases.

The general routine has five moves, and the first is the one beginners miss. **Move the tool's sliders first**, so there is an effect to limit; Skylum's instruction is to adjust the tool and then open its Masking tab ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/masking-options)). Open the tool from the Tools tab (`E`), set for example Structure AI's Amount, then click **Masking** inside that same tool. Choose one of the mask types listed. Draw or click on the photo. Then press `/` to show the mask as a red overlay and see exactly where the effect applies ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/something-useful/keyboard-shortcut-keys)); press `/` again to hide it. Finally soften the edge with **Feather**, one of the mask actions, so the boundary does not show ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/masking-options/mask-actions)).

| Mask type | Use it for | How to make it | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Object Select AI** | The animal, a person, a tree | Hover over the photo: a recognised object lights up. Click it. Click other areas to add them; choose **Subtract** and click to remove a part | [Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/masking-options/object-select-ai) |
| **Mask AI** | The whole sky, the ground, water, plants, mountains | Luminar lists the elements it found (up to nine kinds; none is "animal"). Click one or several, for example the sky. Tidy the result with the Brush | [Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/masking-options/mask-ai) |
| **Linear Gradient** (`G`) | A sky that should darken gradually toward the top; a horizon glow | Drag across the horizon. The effect fades along the drag. Refine afterwards with the Brush | [Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/masking-options/linear-gradient-mask) |
| **Radial** (`R`) | A round area: a face, the Milky Way core | Drag an ellipse. The inner circle sets the size, the outer one the softness. **Standard** protects the centre and affects the outside; **Inverted** affects the inside | [Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/masking-options/using-a-radial-mask) |
| **Brush** (`B`) | Anything irregular; fixing the other masks | Choose **Paint** and brush over the area; choose **Erase** to remove. **Size** (`[` and `]`), **Softness** (`Shift+[` and `Shift+]`) and **Strength** set the brush. Painted areas show red | [Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/masking-options/brush-mask) |
| **Luminosity** | "Only the bright parts" or "only the dark parts" | Move the single range slider from dark to light and watch the live preview | [Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/masking-options/luminosity-masking) |
| **Color** | "Only the orange" | Click a colour in the photo, then widen or narrow it with **Range**. Skylum lists this mask among upgrade-gated tools, so it may be absent | [Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/masking-options/color-masking) |

Three mask actions do most of the remaining work ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/masking-options/mask-actions)). **Invert** (`Cmd+I`) flips the mask: select the animal, invert, and the tool now acts on everything except the animal, which is how the "background" steps in the tables are done. **Copy** and **Paste** move a mask from one tool to another, so the animal is selected once and reused. **Clear** removes the mask and starts again; **Fill** covers the whole picture, useful before erasing a part with the Brush.

A first exercise on the lion (DSC03658) makes it concrete. Open Structure AI and raise Amount until the fur gains texture. Click Masking, choose Object Select AI, click the lion. Press `/`: the lion should be red and the thorn bush not. If part of the bush is red, use Subtract or the Brush on Erase. Apply Feather, press `/` again, and hold `\` to compare with the original. Then open Color, lower Saturation a little, click Masking, Paste the lion mask and press `Cmd+I`: the background, and only the background, becomes quieter.

Three limits are worth knowing before starting. Skylum's pages do not show where each button sits in version 1.29, so labels are reliable but positions must be found in the app. Each tool carries one mask; Skylum states that the same tool can be added more than once with its own mask ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/editing-tools/layers)) but does not describe the gesture, which is the first item in the owner's verification list below. And nothing is documented about mask accuracy on fur, small animals or night scenes, so always look at the red overlay at 100% and expect to correct edges by hand.

Other basic actions the tables assume, with their documented paths: the list of tools already applied, and the history, is the **Edits** tab ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/saving-and-exporting-files/saving-adjustments-in-a-catalog)); the original is shown by holding `\` or with the Visibility icon ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/organizing-images/marking-favorite-and-rejected-images)); the histogram is View > Show Histogram and `J` toggles clipping warnings ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/how-to-use-luminar-for-desktop/catalog/histogram)); to copy an edit to other frames, select the edited frame, add the others with Shift-click or Cmd-click, then right-click → Adjustments > Sync Adjustments or `Shift+Cmd+S` ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/how-to-use-luminar-for-desktop/catalog/syncing-adjustments)); a safe trial version of a photo is a virtual copy, `Cmd+'` ([Skylum](https://support.skylum.com/organizing-images/virtual-copies)).

### What only the owner can verify in the app

Skylum's documentation is silent on nearly everything quantitative and on all genre technique, so the guide's numbers and its night workflow must stay labelled as its own. The following need a hands-on check in 1.29.x, ordered by how much guide text depends on them. First, the exact gesture for adding a second instance of a tool with its own mask; if it works, chapter 5 can present a second Develop with Mask AI or Object Select AI as the precise method and Dodge & Burn as the painterly one. Second, whether Remove Dust Spots results can be synced or copied between frames, tested on the three dusty frames. Third, which gated tools the owner's licence actually shows. Fourth, whether Upscale, GenErase and GenExpand, which launch from the Catalog and write a new file, work from the edited rendering or the original. Fifth, the export dialog: Quality scale, Sharpen choices, colour-space list and whether TIFF export offers 16-bit, which the stacking route needs. Sixth, whether Temperature and Tint on a JPEG show kelvin or relative units, and each slider's range. Seventh, whether HDR Merge and Panorama Stitching accept JPEG. Eighth, Noiseless AI at its suggested level on a 100% star-field crop and on the ISO 12800 elephants. Ninth, how the display compares with Preview on the owner's Mac, given Skylum's sRGB statement. Tenth, what `;` Compare shows and what Copy/Paste Adjustments carries, since Skylum documents only the shortcut names. Outside Luminar, the Starry Landscape Stacker trial will settle JPEG input in minutes.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of the guide more than its recipes. The guide treats difficult frames as rescue jobs with a tool for each fault; the evidence says the decisive choices for this set are made before any slider moves: which of a thousand dusk frames to keep, whether a floodlit elephant becomes a partially corrected colour picture or a black-and-white one, whether a Milky Way sequence exists to stack, and at what size each crop will be seen. Fixed white balance, the cause of the set's casts, is also its best batch-editing asset.

Two things remain unknown and would move the recommendations if tested. Nobody has published a controlled comparison of AI denoising, stacking or upscaling on camera JPEGs, so the owner's own trials on DSC01526 and DSC00762 will be better evidence than anything cited here. And the guide's promise that every Luminar claim is verifiable now has a sharper boundary: Skylum verifies what tools exist and what their controls are called, and nothing about how far to push them on an 8-bit file. Stating that boundary once, clearly, is worth more than any single corrected line.
